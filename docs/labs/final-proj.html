Final Project

From raw data to a model you can test next semester

Overview

The midterm gave you one messy dataset. The final asks you to find your own: a large public dataset that can answer a question nobody has answered yet. You’ll take it from raw file to model: wrangle it into an analytic dataset, describe and visualize it, and build a regression model step by step.

This is the first half of a two-semester project. This semester you handle the data and the statistics. Next semester, in Applied Research Methods, you build the theory, preregister, run the confirmatory analysis, and write the paper.

ImportantDates

Final package due: Friday, December 11 at 11:59 pm

250 points, 25% of your course grade. Four milestones along the way are worth 100 of those points; see the timeline below.

What you’ll need

  • Download the project template (.zip). Unzip it, open project.Rproj, and read START_HERE.md first. It walks through every milestone.

Everything is a Quarto file, like your labs:

File What it does When
split.qmd Splits your data 30/70 and fingerprints the holdout M2
wrangle.qmd Raw data to analysis-ready: audit, your wrangling steps, codebook M2
memo.qmd Your memo: wrangling, description, model building, and the plan for the holdout M3, final
README.md Dataset, holdout statement, open questions, AI use Final

Submit each milestone to myCourses unless it says otherwise.

NoteWhy the 30/70 split?

On the first day of M2 you split your data: 30% to explore this semester, and 70% held out, untouched, until next semester. Anything you find in the 30% is a hypothesis. The real test runs on data nobody has seen, which is what makes a preregistration mean something. So the one rule that matters most: never open data/holdout.csv.

NoteWorking in JAMOVI?

This project has to be done in R and Quarto. The holdout and the reproducibility swap only work if every step is code that someone else can rerun. If that’s a problem for you, talk to me before M1.

For this project you will:

  1. Find a public dataset that can answer a novel research question.
  2. Split it 30/70 before looking at any outcome, and timestamp the split on Zenodo.
  3. Wrangle the 30% into an analytic dataset: fix missing-data codes, apply exclusions, and create the variables you need, all in code anyone can rerun.
  4. Describe your sample and every analysis variable with descriptive statistics, correlations, and visualizations.
  5. Build a statistical model step by step, comparing models and checking diagnostics.
  6. Plan how the model will be tested on the 70% next semester.
  7. Reproduce: have a classmate render your project from scratch.

Large public datasets rarely arrive analysis-ready, so most of the work, and most of the points, are in wrangling and describing.

The goal is a publication-ready project. Whether it gets published depends on things outside this course, including the results, so no project is promised publication, and your grade does not depend on it being published.

Choosing a dataset

Your dataset must meet every requirement below. If it fails one, pick another; don’t argue around it.

  • Public and de-identified. No data you collected for this course, no data needing new consent, no restricted-use files. (Thesis data: see Policies.)
  • Licensed for reuse and publication. Record the license. If redistribution isn’t allowed, your code downloads the data instead of storing it.
  • Documented. A codebook or questionnaire that defines every variable you use and how it was coded.
  • Large enough to split. At least n = 1,000 usable units of analysis (if less, please confirm with me), so the 70% holdout can detect a small-to-moderate effect. Units are usually participants, but items count in item-level designs (for example, 40,000 words in a lexicon megastudy).
  • Fit for regression. One outcome, at least one focal predictor, and theory-motivated covariates or a moderator. If you have another type of analysis in mind, please check with me.
  • Not already answered. This is the requirement most likely to sink a paper later, so it is checked hardest. Search Google Scholar, other databases such as PsycINFO, and the repository’s publication list for papers using this dataset. List the 3 to 5 closest papers and state in one sentence what your question adds. “Same question, different dataset” or “same dataset, different question” can work. Same question and same dataset does not.

The assignment

M1 — Dataset pitch (Fri 11/6, 20 points)

Purpose: choose a dataset that can answer a new question before you invest any work in it. No code.

Submit: one Word or PDF file, at most one page per candidate plus references. Start with one line saying whether you’re working alone or with a partner (and who).

For each of two candidate datasets:

  • name, link, and citation;
  • license, and whether redistribution is allowed;
  • n (usable units of analysis) and who the participants are;
  • the outcome, focal predictor, and covariates you would use, with how each is measured;
  • a draft research question in one sentence;
  • a check against the dataset requirements (public, licensed, documented, large enough, fit for regression), one line each;
  • the novelty check: the 3 to 5 closest papers, and one sentence on what your question adds.
Graded on Points
Both candidates fully described 8
Honest check against every requirement 6
A real novelty check: papers found and the contribution stated 6

I approve one candidate or send you back with what to fix. Do not split any data until you have approval.

M2 — Split and wrangle (Sun 11/15, 35 points)

Purpose. First, lock away the holdout (70%) so it stays untouched until the final analysis. Then turn your exploration sample (30%) into a clean analytic dataset. For large public datasets, this can be a big job

1. Split (split.qmd)

  • Put the raw data file in data/raw/.

  • In split.qmd, fill in the three settings lines: file path, random seed, ID column, or NA if each person has one row.

  • Render split.qmd before you look at your outcome variable. It’s fine to read the dataset’s documentation and check variable names (you need the ID column name). Do not make summaries, tables or plots of the outcome in the full data.

  • If each person has more than one row (e.g., trials, visits, waves), set the ID column. That keeps all of a person’s rows together, so no one appears in both the exploration sample and the holdout.

  • Rendering creates data/explore.csv, data/holdout.csv, and holdout_checksum.txt. From here on, work only with data/explore.csv. Don’t open the holdout.

  • At the top of wrangle.qmd, add one sentence: “I rendered split.qmd before examining the outcome variable.” This works on the honor system, and we take your word for it.

2. Timestamp (Zenodo)

  • Log in to zenodo.org, click New upload, and upload only split.qmd and holdout_checksum.txt. No data files.

  • Fill in the title and author, then click Publish. Saving a draft is not enough: only a published record gets a DOI. Published records can’t be edited, so check your files first.

  • Copy the DOI into the README at the root of your project.

Why: the checksum is a fingerprint of your holdout file. If the holdout were ever re-drawn or changed, its fingerprint wouldn’t match. Zenodo’s date and DOI let anyone confirm the holdout hasn’t changed since you published. Because the data are public, this is a commitment, not proof that you never looked at the full dataset.

3. Audit (wrangle.qmd)

Working in the exploration sample, check every variable you’ll use, including the outcome, for:

  • Range: are the minimum and maximum possible?

  • Missing-data codes: values like -9, 99 or 999 that mean “missing” but look like numbers.

  • Anything odd: duplicate IDs, unexpected categories, numbers stored as text, very skewed or nearly empty variables.

Write up what you found: a short table or a sentence per variable will work. The audit is where you find problems. Wrangling is where you fix them.

4. Wrangle (wrangle.qmd)

Each problem from your audit, and anything your research question needs, becomes a step. Give each step its own heading and one sentence on why you did it. Depending on your data, steps might include:

  • Recoding missing-data codes to NA

  • Exclusions, recording how many people remain after each one

  • Joining files (e.g., merging a demographics file with a responses file by ID)

  • Aggregating trials to people (e.g., one mean reaction time per person instead of 200 rows)

  • Reverse-scoring items

  • Scale scores with a missing-item rule (e.g., “average the items if the person answered at least 80% of them; otherwise NA”)

  • Turning numeric codes into labeled categories (1/2 → “control”/”treatment”)

The Toolbox section at the end of the wrangle.qmd file has code to help.

5. Required outputs (end of wrangle.qmd)

However you get there, wrangle.qmd must end with:

  • A check of every variable you created: e.g., a crosstab of each recoded variable against the original, the range of each new variable, and a few rows checked by hand for computed scores.

  • The saved analytic dataset in data/processed/analytic.csv.

  • A codebook for every variable in the analytic dataset (raw and created), with name, description, type, values or range, missing codes, and, for created variables, how it was made.

Submit: the rendered wrangle.qmd (Word Doc, on myCourses ) and your Zenodo DOI/URL. You don’t need to submit split.qmd separately because it’s on Zenodo.

Graded on

Criterion Points
Statement that the split was rendered before examining the outcome; seed recorded; split by ID if people have multiple rows; Zenodo record published with only split.qmd and the checksum; DOI in README 6
Every analysis variable audited, with findings written up 8

M3 — Describe and build an initial model (Mon 11/30, 30 points)

Purpose: know your sample and variables before you model them, then fit and check a first model.

  1. In memo.qmd, replace the example variable names with yours. For a 0/1 outcome or many rows per person, use the logistic or multilevel line shown under Model 1.
  2. Descriptives: a table for every analysis variable: n, M, SD, range, and skew for continuous variables; counts and percentages for categorical ones.
  3. Correlations: a correlation matrix of the continuous analysis variables, shown as a table and a heat map.
  4. Visualizations: the distribution of every analysis variable (histograms or bar charts), plus at least one plot of the focal relationship.
  5. Initial model: fit Model 1, the theory-driven starting model. Check its diagnostics (residuals vs. fitted, Q-Q plot, influential cases) and interpret it in a few sentences.

Submit: the rendered memo. Rough prose is fine.

Graded on Points
Descriptives for every analysis variable, continuous and categorical 8
Correlation matrix and heat map 5
Distribution plots for every variable, plus the focal relationship 9
Initial model fit, diagnostics checked, and interpreted 8

After M3, finish building the model: add predictors in planned blocks, compare the models, and write the plan for the holdout as the memo’s last section.

M4 — Reproducibility swap (Thu 12/3, in class, 15 points)

Purpose: find out whether your project works on someone else’s computer, while there’s still time to fix it.

Before class: copy your project folder, delete data/holdout.csv from the copy, and zip it.

In class: trade zips with your assigned swap partner. Render their project without help from them or from AI tools. Record every problem before you try to fix it.

Submit (end of class): a swap report on your partner’s project. For each problem: what broke, the file and line, and a suggested fix. Also: did it render on the first try, and do the numbers match their memo?

Graded on Points
Thorough, specific swap report on your partner’s project 8
Your project renders on your partner’s machine (partial credit for each problem fixed by Dec 11) 4
A short list in your README of what you fixed after the swap, due with the final 3

Final package (Fri 12/11, 150 points)

Submit one zipped project folder, including the unopened data/holdout.csv. It must render from a clean R session.

Tip

Before you submit, restart R (Session → Restart R) and render split.qmd, wrangle.qmd, and memo.qmd in that order. split.qmd should say MATCH. If anything fails, it will fail on my computer too.

The heart of the submission is the memo: 3 to 4 pages of text, rendered to Word or PDF. Tables and figures don’t count toward the page limit. It follows the project’s arc (question and data, wrangling, description, model building, plan for the holdout) and claims no confirmatory finding. Writing is graded for clarity; argument and framing belong to next semester.

Policies

  • Alone or in pairs. You can work alone or with one partner whose interests overlap with yours. Expectations are the same either way. Declare your choice at M1; pairs can’t split or form after M2, because partners share one split and holdout. Partners submit under one name, are graded together, and each adds a contribution statement to the README. Swap partners for M4 are assigned separately, whether you work alone or in a pair.
  • Thesis or dissertation data. Allowed only if you meet all four conditions at M1:
    1. Written approval from your advisor, including agreement on authorship of anything published from the project.
    2. Confirmation that your IRB protocol allows course use and sharing de-identified data with a classmate. If it doesn’t, your swap partner works from a synthetic copy (for example, made with the synthpop package).
    3. A research question that is not a specific thesis or dissertation chapter.
    4. Outcome data you haven’t analyzed yet, so the split still works. If you have, the holdout requirement is waived and your plan is framed as exploratory.
  • Authorship. Papers that go to submission will list authors by CRediT contribution roles, with a faculty co-author responsible for the paper after the spring semester ends. Partners agree on first-author order, in writing, before writing begins.
  • Open science. This semester, only the split script and checksum are public, on Zenodo. Working files stay on university storage or in a private GitHub repository. At publication, the full project goes to ResearchBox, which archives public boxes to Zenodo with a DOI: code under an open license, materials, and data where its license allows. ResearchBox requires a university email address to register.
  • Data ethics. Use only de-identified data under its stated license. Never try to identify participants or link records to outside sources.
  • Late work. Milestones build on each other, so a late milestone delays your feedback. Talk to me before a deadline, not after.

Use of AI tools

The syllabus policy on generative AI applies. For this project specifically, use AI tools the way you’d use a knowledgeable classmate. You are responsible for everything you submit, and you should be able to explain every line of your code and every decision in your plan.

Allowed:

  • debugging R code, as long as you can explain what each line does;
  • explaining error messages, statistical concepts, or model output;
  • editing your own writing for clarity (minimally).

Not allowed:

  • Giving an AI tool data/holdout.csv, or the full dataset after your split. Uploading the holdout counts as opening it. Share only a summary of it.
  • Letting an AI tool make analytic decisions you can’t justify yourself: exclusions, covariates, or model choice. Every decision in your plan for the holdout needs a reason in your own words.
  • Citing papers you haven’t read. AI tools invent references and can misstate findings. Your novelty check and memo can only cite papers you have opened and read.
  • Using AI to fix your partner’s project during the M4 swap. You need to identify the problems on your own. The goal of the swap is to test the files and highlight problems, not fix them.
  • Uploading open, thesis, or dissertation data to any AI tool, unless your IRB protocol or data use agreement explicitly allows it.

Disclose: your README has an “AI use” line naming each tool and what you used it for (for example, “Claude: debugging the scoring code in wrangle.qmd; tightening the Limitations paragraph”). This is the acknowledgment note the syllabus requires. “None” is a fine answer. Disclosed use is never penalized; undisclosed use falls under the course’s academic integrity policy.

This log also prepares you for publication: journals have their own AI rules, AI tools can’t be listed as authors, and most journals ask for a disclosure like this one.
